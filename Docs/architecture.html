Memoir watches what is on your screen, remembers it, and answers questions about it. Everything below is the real system: the numbers are measured, the failures are ones that actually shipped and got caught.

01

The shape

What the product is, and the constraints that decided everything else

Memoir is a memory, not a chatbot. It captures the text of whatever app you are looking at, stores it locally, and lets you ask "what was that thing?" a week later. The hard part is not the model. The hard part is that a memory which is confidently wrong is worse than no memory at all.

Constraint

Zero dependencies

No packages. Apple frameworks and SQLite only. A memory you trust cannot have a supply chain you do not control.

Constraint

Nothing leaves

Local by default. Tested structurally: a counting URLProtocol fails the suite if any request is even attempted.

Constraint

Text only

Accessibility tree, never screenshots. No pixels, no OCR, no video buffer sitting on disk.

The design axiom. The model's job is to phrase, not to know. Every fact in an answer must already exist in the evidence that was handed to it. Everything in this document is downstream of that one sentence.

03

LLM boundaries

What each brain is allowed to do and, more importantly, what it is not

The single most useful thing we did was stop asking "which model is best" and start asking "what is the smallest thing that can be trusted with this step". Four tiers, and work falls to the cheapest one that can be held accountable for it.

Tier 0 · no model
RulesOnlyBrain

Deterministic SQL and string assembly. Always available, always the floor: the product never says nothing.

Count, sum, sort, list
Time arithmetic over sessions
Cannot phrase naturally
Structurally cannot hallucinate, so it is exempt from the grounding guards
Tier 1 · on device
Apple Foundation Models · ~3B · 4k ctx

Free, private, offline. Also small enough to pattern-complete nonsense, so it is allowed only narrow, bounded jobs.

Classify into a closed set (@Generable)
Phrase an answer over supplied evidence
Never asked to recall from its own weights
Never trusted with arithmetic
Never allowed to name a fact absent from context
Tier 2 · local network
Qwen3-30B · Mac mini over Tailscale

Same house, same trust boundary, so it does not sit behind the cloud consent gate. Open-ended synthesis, and the judge for graded evals.

Multi-hop reasoning over a long packet
Grading eval answers as a judge
Unavailable when the mini sleeps: must degrade, never fail
Tier 3 · cloud
Anthropic API · consent-gated

Off unless explicitly enabled. Three separate tests exist purely to prove it can never be selected by accident.

Best quality when the user opts in
Never on fallback
Never on retry
Never when every local brain is unavailable
The boundary that matters most. A 3B model given an open question invents a plausible answer. The same model given "here are six captures, phrase what they say" is reliable. We did not make the model smarter. We made its job smaller.
04

Routing, and how the threshold was chosen

The cascade is easy. The calibrated confidence signal is the whole game.

Every question is classified into one of five categories before anything else happens, because the category decides which context gets built and which tier answers. Two stages, cheapest first.

Stage 1: free

Sentence embedding of the question, cosine against a centroid per category, built from a handful of example phrasings. ~4 ms, no tokens, no network.

margin = best − second_best

The margin is the point: it is the model telling you how much to trust itself.

Stage 2: expensive

Only when the margin is thin. A @Generable-constrained classifier that is structurally incapable of returning an invalid category. ~15 s.

If escalation fails, we return the unsure stage-1 answer with its low margin attached, rather than losing the question. Degrade, never drop.

The measurement that set the threshold

16 real questions across all five categories. Stage 1 got 14 right. The interesting part is not the accuracy. It is that both mistakes landed among the three lowest margins in the set.

correct · n=14
margin 0.058 ────────────────────────── 0.278
wrong · n=2
margin 0.037 ─── 0.070
threshold = 0.075 · catches BOTH errors · sends only 2 of 14 correct answers to the model unnecessarily

Result: ~87% of questions answered in ~4 ms, and the two the router got wrong both escalate for a second opinion. The distributions overlap slightly, so no threshold is perfect. This is a precision/recall dial, not a boundary, and it is documented as one in the source.

Transferable. A cascade that escalates on "parsing failed" is a fixed rule and cannot know when it is wrong. A cascade that escalates on a calibrated confidence margin knows when it does not know. That difference is the load-bearing part of the FrugalGPT / RouteLLM literature, and it is the part most implementations skip.
05

Retrieval

Two searches, because each one fails in a way the other does not

Keyword · SQLite FTS5 + BM25

Exact, cheap, explainable. Fails on paraphrase: you remember "wrapped in an sms app", the post said "wrapped into a nice sms app layer", and literal search returns nothing.

FTS5 joins terms with AND by default: every word had to appear. One unusual word in your question and the whole query returns empty.
Switched to OR + stopword removal. BM25 then ranks by how many terms hit and how rare they are, which is what you wanted all along.

Semantic · NLEmbedding, on device

512-dim sentence vectors, ~4 ms each, embedded once at write time. Catches paraphrase. Fails on long pages, badly.

A capture containing the exact phrase scored 0.306 as a whole, below unrelated pages at 0.44. Most of its characters were browser chrome, and a sentence embedding averages meaning away.
Split into overlapping 220-char passages; a capture is represented by its best passage, not its average. Coarse whole-capture vectors shortlist, passages rank.

The gap nobody noticed: it was built and never plugged in

The semantic index was complete, tested, and backfilled. And the context builder never called it. Retrieval was keyword-only, and the failure it caused looked exactly like a ranking bug:

Q: "what was that repo about screen memory" → mem0ai/mem0
BM25 is right on the words: mem0's title literally says "Universal memory layer". screenpipe's does not.
Q: "what was that repo about screen memory" → n1ghtjar/Afterglance: AI-powered screen memory
One line wiring the index in. Words rank the wrong page first; meaning ranks the right one.

Presence in the packet is not prominence in it

Wiring the index in was not enough. The right page was now in the context (at position 11, below the wrong one), and the model reads top-down. It still answered mem0ai/mem0 three times in five.

Attempt 1: append

Semantic hits after keyword hits.

3/5 wrong. Present but buried.

Attempt 2: fuse by rank

Reciprocal rank fusion: Σ 1/(k + rank), k=60. Uses only position, which is the one thing both searches express on the same scale.

Still mem0, and correctly so. Ranks (1,2) beat (3,1). Both searches genuinely do like mem0.

Attempt 3: a third signal

Exact phrase search. "screen memory" appears verbatim in Afterglance's page and nowhere in mem0's.

4/4 correct. One first-place finish in a list carrying information neither of the others had.
Why not just normalise the scores? A BM25 of 8.2 and a cosine of 0.41 have no common scale. Squashing them into one number would be a fiction dressed as arithmetic. RRF discards the magnitudes and keeps only what is genuinely comparable: where each result placed in its own list. When two rankers disagree, the fix is rarely a better weighting; it is usually a third signal that settles it. Here that signal was adjacency: two words the asker put side by side belong together, and the same two words scattered across a page do not.
question: "what was that repo about screen memory" phrase "screen memory" → Afterglance ① ← only this list knows keyword screen OR memory → mem0 ① · other ② · Afterglance ③ semantic best-passage cos → Afterglance ① · mem0 ② ───────────────────────────────────────────────────── fused Afterglance ① · mem0 ② · other ③

Why brute force, and when to stop

Measured against the real corpus, not guessed:

60-day retention≈ 24k vectors≈ 47 MB≈ 25 ms / search
one year≈ 146k vectors≈ 285 MB≈ 150 ms / search
ceiling 100k vectors. Above it, a search becomes perceptible and an approximate index finally earns its dependency. The code logs a warning when it gets there.

A vector database would save perhaps 20 ms at our size while adding a dependency to a project whose reliability comes from having none. Retention caps growth, so the corpus does not grow without bound. The decision is written down with its expiry condition, which is the only honest way to defer one.

06

Grounding guards

Five enforcement passes. Every one exists because a real answer was wrong.

Asked "what do I owe anyone" against a database with zero commitments, and handed Commitments: none recorded as the literal first line of its context, the model answered "You owe someone $100." Moving the negative to the top did not fix it. Strengthening the prompt did not fix it. A small model pattern-completes, and no amount of instruction reliably stops it.

So grounding is enforced, not requested. The answer is parsed after generation and every claim is checked against the evidence that produced it. A claim that is not supported means the whole answer is thrown away and replaced with an honest refusal.
ungroundedNumbersfabrication

Every figure in the answer must appear in the evidence.

"You owe someone $100" (against zero commitments)

Then it got defeated:

A page reading "100% local, 100% private" was grounding "$100"
Currency became part of the token. A bare digit can no longer ground a monetary claim. Small counts and years stay free: that is how a model says "two things".
unsupportedHostsinvention

Any domain named must exist in the evidence.

Asked about "lmuendeild" (a typo for lumenfield), it answered "You were on https://lmuendeild.ai", inventing a domain by echoing the typo back as fact.
Hosts are extracted from the answer and checked against the context. Subdomains of a known host pass; scheme and www. normalise away.
unsupportedActionsfalse memory

Claiming the user did something requires attribution in the evidence.

"You created a note" (nothing of the kind had happened)

Then it got defeated:

A Finder column header reading "Date Created" satisfied the verb check
Now requires actual attribution ("you created", "created by"), not the bare verb anywhere on screen.
isEcho + refusal stringsnon-answer

An answer that restates the question, or that emits a canned refusal phrase while pretending to have content, is not an answer.

Q: "what was the motion website" → A: "The motion website."
Caught and replaced with a real refusal, which is honest and therefore more useful than a confident echo.

Note the pattern in three of the four: the first version of the guard was defeated by real screen text. Guards written against your imagination fail. Guards written against your logs survive.

A fifth guard: the answer that is true and empty

isTautologyno information
Q: "what was that repo about screen memory" → A: "The repo was about screen memory."

Every guard passed. Nothing was fabricated, no figure invented, and it is not a literal echo because the word order differs. It is simply, perfectly, empty: the question in the indicative mood.

If the reply introduces no content word the asker did not already supply, it carries no information. Bounded to short replies: a long answer that reuses the question's nouns is elaborating on them, which is exactly its job.

Rejection falls to the floor, not to silence

Asked "what was I doing an hour ago", the model answered "at 16:00" when the timeline said 16:49. The figure guard correctly rejected the invented time, and the user got "I do not have anything recorded about that" about an hour Memoir had a complete record of. The guard was right and the outcome was still wrong.

A rejected answer now falls to Tier 0, which reads the store directly and is grounded by construction. Only when the floor has nothing either does Memoir actually refuse. A guard should remove the lie, not the answer.

The same mistake, three layers deep

That fallback then had to be corrected twice, and both corrections were the same mistake wearing a different hat: an eagerness to produce content where a refusal was the honest answer.

Layer 1: the floor recites

Tier 0 always answers; that is what makes it a floor. So as a fallback it offered its status report to a question it had not addressed.

"what was I doing in 1995" → "6 open commitments, 1 past due. Recently in play: …"

Worse than a refusal, because it sounds like an answer. Fixed: the fallback path may decline, while answer() still always speaks.

Layer 2: the floor matches too loosely

Its entity matcher used a bare substring test.

"what was I checking on lmuendeild last" matched the repo "cadenroe/last30days-skill" and offered it as the closest thing.

A fragment inside a longer token is a coincidence, not a match. Fixed with whole-word matching. A confident coincidence is the worst thing a memory can produce.

Layer 3: I rebuilt the bug

The model says "I cannot answer that question" about half the time on one question. I treated that as a failure to route around, and let the floor answer from the retrieved evidence instead.

"what was I doing in 1995" → "The closest things I have are these: Google Chrome …"

Layer 1's exact failure, reintroduced one layer down, by me, minutes after fixing it.

A declining model is usually right. The final design does not route around it. It translates it. Same verdict, said in Memoir's voice rather than the model's, because "I cannot answer that question" is not a sentence this product says. The fallback-to-floor is reserved for the genuinely different case: the model did answer, and stated something its evidence could not support.

The dead end was reverted rather than kept, including a matchedCaptureIDs field threaded through the context packet to support it. Shipping it unused would have left an API whose only justification was an approach that did not work.

Related tokenizer bug found the same way: 16:00 was being split into 16 and 00, and rejected because neither half appeared in the evidence. A bare 00 is not a claim anyone makes. A clock time is now one token, so it is grounded when that time was on the timeline and not otherwise.

Where guards are the wrong tool entirely

A grounding guard asks one question: is this claim supported by the evidence? That is exactly the wrong question for a credential.

Q: "what is my password for github" → A: "Your password for github is \"login\"."

Confident, wrong, and assembled from the text around a login form. Secure fields are never read and anything resembling a secret is dropped at capture, but "never captured" is not the same as "never answered". And had a real credential ever reached the corpus, a grounding guard would have waved it through as supported.

So three question classes are now refused deterministically, before retrieval and before any model runs:

Credentials

"I won't repeat passwords, keys or codes, not even if one crossed the screen."

Matches possessive and interrogative forms only, so "that article about password managers" stays answerable.

Private browsing

"I don't record private or incognito windows at all."

Structural, not a lookup. The honest answer does not depend on the database.

Prediction

"I only have a record of what has already happened."

The real failure was "You will likely continue working on your AI-native product." The word "tomorrow" alone must not trigger it: "what's due tomorrow" is a real question.

A guarantee cannot be delegated to a classifier. "Never reveal a credential" is a security property. A router that is right 87% of the time is excellent routing and unacceptable security. Know which of your requirements are preferences and which are promises, and implement the promises where no probability is involved.
07

Evals

Unit tests prove the machinery works. Evals prove the answers are good. Different question.

Shape of a case

{ "q": "what was that tweet about claude skills in an sms app", "expect": "Names the tweet's subject.", "mustContainAny": ["claude skills", "sms app", "mirafenn"], "mustNotContain": ["don't have", "no record", ...] }

Three assertion kinds: mustContain (all), mustContainAny (at least one), mustNotContain (none). The expect field is prose for the human reading a failure: it says what a good answer looks like, so you can tell "not captured yet" from "answered wrongly".

The six groups · 48 cases

recall11 · find a thing seen days ago
resumption8 · reload where you left off
accounting6 · arithmetic over sessions
honesty12 · must refuse; passes on any database
quality3 · reads like an answer, not a dump
edge8 · empty, malformed, adversarial, prompt injection

Only honesty and edge are portable. The rest depend on one person's captured corpus: a real limitation, stated in the runner's own help text rather than hidden.

The rule that made evals trustworthy

What went wrong

I reported a recall regression and a resumption regression to the user. Neither was real. I had drawn a conclusion from a single noisy run.

The same code scored 20/23 and 21/23 on consecutive runs. One case passed twice, then failed.

The fix

Every case runs N times (default 3). A case passes only if it passes every run.

PASS = 3/3 · FLAKY = 1–2/3 · FAIL = 0/3
Flaky is not passing. An answer that is right two times in three is a bug you have not characterised yet.
Generative systems are stochastic; your test harness must be statistical. Grading a rate rather than a coin flip is the difference between measuring and guessing. This is the single change that most improved our ability to tell whether we were making progress.
08

Drift and feedback loops

The worst bug of the project, and why it took seven wrong diagnoses

A memory system reads its own output. That closes a loop, and closed loops in AI systems fail in ways that look nothing like their cause.

The self-echo death spiral

Memoir filters out captures that are its own output; otherwise, its answer appears on screen, gets captured, and pollutes the next answer. Sensible. Here is what actually happened:

01 · sourceA tweet is on screen. Captured correctly.
02 · answerYou ask about it. Memoir answers, correctly, by quoting the tweet.
03 · logThat answer is appended to the ask log.
04 · poisonThe ask log becomes the self-echo fingerprint set.
05 · erasureThe ORIGINAL tweet now matches a fingerprint and is suppressed as "Memoir's own output".
Answering a question once made it permanently unanswerable. The memory erased whatever it successfully recalled.
Fix: only a capture that postdates an answer can be an echo of it. Self-echo became time-aware, and the loop broke.

The second-order echo: debugging a memory in front of the memory

The self-echo guard catches Memoir's own answers reappearing on screen. It cannot catch a conversation about those answers, and those conversations quote the failures verbatim. Here is the actual evidence packet Memoir built for a recall question:

Claude: "…lmuendeild last? Answer: You were on https://lmuendeild.ai at 17:40"
Claude: "Recall regressed: what url was the motion website → https://mem0ai/mem0"

Those are bug reports. Memoir read them and served the documented wrong answers back as fact.

And every grounding guard passed, correctly. The host guard asks "does this domain appear in the evidence?", and by then the invented domain genuinely did, because we had pasted it into an assistant window while debugging. The guard was not broken. The corpus was poisoned with the guard's own failure log.

Symptom

Three recall evals failing, each returning a specific, plausible, completely wrong URL or repo.

Apparent cause

Retrieval precision. Ranking. Typo correction echoing back. All wrong.

Actual cause

Assistant conversations were being cited as evidence. One filter, already written for the semantic index, was never applied to keyword retrieval. All three failures fixed at once.

What you asked is not what you saw. Only the latter is evidence. Titles still flow to the resumption timeline: "you were in Claude at 16:49" is true and useful. It is the body text that may never be cited as fact. If you build a system that observes, assume it will observe you working on it, and decide in advance what that is worth.

Why it took seven wrong diagnoses

Search was blamed. Ranking was blamed. Embeddings were blamed. Every one was a plausible story told from the outside. The bug was found in one step by adding a trace to the context-builder loop that printed why each capture was dropped. It printed echo=true.

The lesson that cost the most. In a probabilistic pipeline you cannot reason your way to a root cause from the output: every stage is a plausible suspect and none of them can be eliminated by argument. Instrument the decision, not the result. Log why each candidate was rejected, and the bug names itself.

The other drift defences

Corrections are permanent

Edit an entity's title and no extraction pass may ever overwrite it, not even a higher-confidence one. Tier-0 invariant. If this breaks, the memory cannot be trusted, and an untrustworthy memory is worse than none.

Consolidation is idempotent

Run extraction three times over the same captures; entity count is identical after runs 2 and 3. Re-processing must never duplicate.

Memory outlives its source

Retention rolls captures off at 60 days but never entities. Provenance degrades to "source expired" rather than dangling: the memory survives, the raw text does not.

Never index a conversation with an assistant

A subtle one. Questions embed near other questions, so a semantic search for "that tweet about claude skills in an sms app" returned eight prompts I had typed to an assistant and not one piece of actual content. What you asked is not what you saw, and only the latter is worth remembering.

10

Lessons

What transfers to any AI-native product

01Shrink the job, not the model

A small model asked an open question invents. The same model asked to phrase supplied evidence is reliable. Most "we need a bigger model" problems are "we gave it too much latitude" problems.

02Enforce, do not request

Anything you put in a prompt is a suggestion. Anything you check after generation is a guarantee. If a property matters, assert it in code.

03Escalate on calibrated confidence

Cheap-first cascades are easy. The value is in the confidence signal that decides when to escalate. Measure its distribution against real inputs before picking a threshold.

04Instrument the decision, not the result

Seven wrong diagnoses from reasoning about outputs; one right one from logging why each candidate was rejected. Log your filters.

05Flaky is failing

Run every eval N times and pass only on N/N. A single run of a stochastic system is an anecdote, and anecdotes produced two regression reports that were false.

06Watch for closed loops

The moment a system reads its own output, you have a feedback loop. Ours quietly erased any memory it had successfully recalled. Ask of every filter: what happens on the second pass?

07Guards must be written against logs

Three of four grounding guards were defeated by real screen text within a day: "100% local" grounded "$100"; a Finder header grounded "you created". Your imagination is not an adversary.

08Defer with an expiry condition

"No vector database" is only defensible because the ceiling is measured, written down, and logged when crossed. A deferred decision without a trigger is just an unmade one.

09Refusal is a feature

"I do not have that" is a correct answer, and 12 of 48 eval cases assert it. A memory that invents is not a memory. Users forgive gaps; they do not forgive fiction.

10Have a floor that needs no model

Tier 0 computes from SQL and cannot hallucinate. Every model can be unavailable, slow, or wrong; the product still answers. Design the degraded path first.

11Know your promises from your preferences

"Never reveal a credential" is a guarantee and cannot be delegated to a classifier that is merely usually right. Implement promises where no probability is involved: before retrieval, before any model.

12Your system will observe you building it

We debugged a memory in front of the memory. It learned our bug reports and served the documented wrong answers back as fact, with every guard passing correctly. If your product ingests the world, it ingests your work on the product too.

13Built is not wired

The semantic index was complete, tested and backfilled. And nothing called it. Retrieval was keyword-only for weeks and the failure looked exactly like a ranking bug. Test that components are reached, not only that they work.

14When two rankers disagree, add a signal

Not a better weighting. Reciprocal rank fusion was correct and still lost, because both rankers genuinely liked the wrong page. The fix was a third list carrying information neither had: exact phrase adjacency.

15A declining model is usually right

"I cannot answer that" is often correct. Routing around it is how a fallback starts fabricating: we reproduced our own just-fixed bug one layer down doing exactly that. Translate the refusal into your product's voice; do not overrule it.

16Watch for the eagerness to answer

Three separate bugs, three layers apart, were one instinct: produce content where a refusal was honest. It reappears every time you add a fallback. Ask of each one: what does this say when it has nothing?
